E14

Ep. 14 · May 23, 2026 · 30 min

The Gofman Case: What Makes a Head of Mossad

When Netanyahu nominated his military secretary, Roman Gofman, to run the Mossad, MESS used the news to ask what makes a good Mossad chief. Yossi answers from experience, with first-hand stories about two of the chiefs he worked for.

  • The nomination. Netanyahu nominates Roman Gofman to succeed Dedi Barnea, and MESS uses the news to look at who has run the Mossad since 1949.

  • The Dagan precedent. Meir Dagan was a controversial pick too, and became a spectacularly successful chief: he opened up Iran to the Mossad and brought women to the forefront.

  • Gofman’s story. Born in Belarus, he learned he was Jewish a few months before immigrating at 14, and the army made him Israeli.

  • Learning on the job. Gofman has no intelligence background. Neither did Danny Yatom, whose botched assassination attempt in Amman cost him the job, in an age when Israeli leaders still took responsibility.

  • A Russian speaker. What a Russian speaker might bring to the job, and why the Mossad kept its distance from the Soviet bloc in Yossi’s day.

  • Picked for loyalty. Gofman and Shin Bet chief David Zini look like loyalty appointments. But Mossad chiefs have long had political ties, Yossi notes, from Isser Harel to the Palmach veterans he worked for.

  • October 1973. On the morning the war began, Yossi walks into his boss’s London apartment and finds the head of the Mossad, Tzvi Zamir, back from a night with an Egyptian agent, phoning Prime Minister Golda Meir. What Zamir says next shows that even he didn’t grasp what was coming.

  • Reading Iran. Yossi was the first Mossad officer to deal with the Iranian revolution. He says few people anywhere understand how Iran’s clerics rule.

  • Lebanon, 1982. Yossi’s first trip to Lebanon, by IDF helicopter during the war, and the Maronite allies the Mossad had worked with since 1975.

  • The chiefs he worked for. Yossi describes the two Mossad heads he worked for directly, Tzvi Zamir and Yitzhak Hofi.

E10

Ep. 10 · March 28, 2026 · 29 min

A Heart Attack, Missile Alerts, and the Iran War

Recorded four weeks into the war with Iran, days after Yossi’s heart attack. The podcast studio at the Tel Aviv public library is closed because of the war, so they record online from their homes. Halfway through, a real missile alert cuts Daniella off mid-question. They stop until it’s over, then come back and pick up the same question.

  • A heart attack in wartime. From home, Yossi describes his mild heart attack, the ambulance crew waiting out a siren, and cornering his doctor in a stairwell during an alert.

  • The Kissinger story. Why Yossi owes his bypass, 30 years ago, to a Henry Kissinger story, and what Kissinger said when they met. Then the drive home: a siren, and a roadside ditch.

  • Before 1979. When Israel and Iran were allies.

  • The alert. A real missile alert cuts the conversation off.

  • After the siren. Yossi explains why bombing won’t topple Iran’s regime.

  • Iran’s strategy. Iran is hitting the world through the price of oil. Too early to call that a success, Yossi says: it has deepened the bad blood with its Arab neighbors and exposed how weak the Gulf states are.

  • Where the war stands. A second front with Hezbollah, a US president who wants out, and war goals mostly out of reach: Israel will have to live with a regime that is emerging to be more extreme.

  • The assassinations. Killing enemy leaders, from Hamas commanders to Ali Khamenei, hasn’t won the war or toppled the regime. Yossi calls it a mistaken strategy.

  • Passover in the safe room. Fifteen people are coming to Daniella’s for Passover dinner. Will they spend part of it in the safe room?

E20

Ep. 20 · August 28, 2026 · 26 min

Not My Zionism

A listener asked how Yossi became a Zionist, and what he makes of the word now. His answer starts in 1950s Washington, DC, with a Jewish education in which the Holocaust took up two sentences. That changes during Adolf Eichmann’s trial. Yossi then turns to Israel today, where the secular Zionism that brought him there is losing ground to a religious one.

  • A listener’s question. A friend in New York asks how Yossi became a Zionist. It starts with a bar mitzvah he found farcical, and no synagogue ever after.

  • 1961. A Columbia student in NYC, alienated from his Jewish surroundings, follows the Eichmann trial, which Ben-Gurion meant as a wake-up call. “He was targeting me… And I bit.”

  • Becoming Israeli. Being Jewish, for Yossi, means being Israeli, whether or not the natives are friendly. He arrives by boat from Greece, learns Hebrew on a kibbutz, and not even weeks in hospital with jaundice shakes his resolve.

  • My Zionism. Zionism is a home for the Jewish people that can protect itself, so there is never another Holocaust. His part was the army and the security community.

  • From October 7 back to 1967. What Yossi thinks went wrong.

  • Religious Zionism. Yossi’s Zionism is secular. The one winning, he says, is national religious, and demography is on its side.

  • The view from America. Whether Zionism is a dirty word is an American issue, not an Israeli one. Yossi’s answer to American Jews: “Get used to it.”

His books include Periphery: Israel’s Search for Middle East Allies and the prize-winning Winners and Losers in the ‘Arab Spring’.
